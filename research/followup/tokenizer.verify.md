## Summary

The recommendation survives: tie the embeddings, put the 20M vocabulary at 4k-8k, skip hash tables and plain byte-level. Every piece of the report's own arithmetic reproduces (Tao extrapolations, the 302M anchors, the Limisiewicz extrapolation, the 20M budget table, the 20-message conversation cost). The problems are in three inferences built on those numbers.

1. **The byte-level penalty is quoted from the wrong arm.** The "characters are 19% worse" number comes from a 148,000-entry *character* vocabulary (96M embedding parameters on a 25M body). The same paper (App. F.5, Figure 25) shows a true byte model (V=256) at about 1.20 bits per byte against 1.06 for BPE at 1e19 FLOPs. That is roughly 13% worse, read off the figure, and the authors attribute the character-vs-byte gap at small compute to embedding cost. Separately, SpaceByte (NeurIPS 2024) roughly matches a 50k-vocab subword Transformer at 1e18-1e19 FLOPs with rule-based (space) patching. So the report's line that byte models reach parity "only with learned patching, at 400M to 8B" is wrong. "No plain byte-level Planck" still stands (a plain byte Transformer is 12-18% worse at 1e19 and "can require roughly 10 times more training FLOPs").
2. **The "optimal compression 4.3-4.8 bytes/token" target does not apply to Planck (claim 5, refuted as a design target).** Limisiewicz et al. say their T* holds where "vocabulary cost is (almost) the same regardless of compression". They also say that Tao-style coupling of compression to embedding size "leads to a preference for smaller vocabularies at low compute and parameter budgets". Planck is in exactly that coupled regime. The report's own OASST numbers show that an in-domain 8k reaches 3.83, not 4.3+. Even an in-domain 49k (4.44) does not reach 4.5. The same paper supplies a better number: with embeddings free, dropping from 4.57 to 3.71 bytes/token cost only 1.7% bits per byte at 1e19.
3. **The in-domain tokenizer advantage is overstated for chat.** Karpathy's "4k ≈ 32k" (8x) is for TinyStories' roughly 1,500-word world. On OASST chat, in-domain training is worth about 1.5x in vocabulary (in-domain 32k = SmolLM2's general 49k = 4.36), and in-domain 8k is 12% behind a general 49k. The "general 8k" the report compares against is Monad's tokenizer, which is itself a domain tokenizer (trained only on SYNTH).

Smaller fixes: MultiHashFormer's "adding 4 layers beats every hash variant" is overstated per task, though the parameter-matched gist holds. The 10M row's embedding share is 8-10.5%, not 5-8%. Only the 8k and 49k arms of the 20M table meet the report's own ±1% total rule. The printed T0 = 18.2 does not reproduce the paper's own 3.69 / 3.33 examples (T0 ≈ 18.5 does). Four of the five checked ledger ideas (L5, L8, L11, L12) have partial prior art.

## Scale misapplications

1. **Compute Optimal Tokenization's T* used as a Planck target (claim 5, bottom line 3).** It was measured at 50M-6.7B and at least 5e18 FLOPs, with compression decoupled from vocabulary cost (BLT). It is applied to a 20M fixed-total model at 4e16-1e18 FLOPs, where compression is paid for in embedding rows. The source warns about exactly this regime.
2. **The 19% "character" penalty used as the byte-level penalty.** It was measured with a 148k-entry character table (96M embedding at the smallest size), while Planck's byte-level arm would have V=256. The same paper's byte curve gives about 13% at 1e19.
3. **Chung and Kim's rare-word result carried below 24K (L7 rationale).** The evidence is at 85M and 450M *non-embedding* bodies (embeddings free), 10-30B tokens, and vocabularies of 24K and up. Whether "smaller vocabulary means better rare-name handling" continues from 24K down to 4-8k, under a fixed total, is untested. The report flags the free-embedding part.
4. **Lopardo's "tie at 70M-class" read as evidence.** It is an argument from parameter share. Their experiments are all OLMo-1B at 2-20B tokens. The actual small-scale evidence for tying is MobileLLM at 125M.
5. **Tao's law below 33M and at about 20 tokens per parameter.** The report flags this. Add that the fitted law is about 1.35x above Tao's own direct 302M measurement, so the law value (8.0k) is an upper-end estimate and the 302M anchor (6.2k) is the better center.
6. **Ferrando's 5-10%+ chat savings.** These come from production tokenizers of 32k-256k re-fit on multilingual LMSYS, and part of the saving is general inefficiency in those tokenizers. They say nothing about what an 8k vocabulary gains from chat-specific training.
7. **MobileLLM-R1's 53% embedding share.** It is a reasoning model distilled with same-vocabulary logit KD from an 8B teacher. It is evidence that a big shared vocabulary can be worth it *when logit KD is used*, not a general statement about tiny-model vocabulary.

## Novelty overclaims (ledger ideas marked untested)

- **L5 (fixed byte-composition input + untied head, with an untied-learned control): partially tried.** Bochkov (TMLR 2025, arXiv 2507.04886), which the report cites, contains the missing control. At 335.8M it trains frozen glyph-derived input embeddings with an independently trained (untied) output head against "baseline model with identical architectures but with a standard, randomly initialized and trainable embedding layer", and reports "nearly identical loss trajectories". That is a fixed-codec-vs-untied-learned comparison with no loss gain. It supports the report's suspicion that Kronecker's +2.5% is mostly the untying. The older lineage (character-composed inputs with a learned word-level output) is Kim et al. 2016 (arXiv 1508.06615, AAAI 2016: character CNN input with word-level prediction, on par with the state of the art on PTB with 60% fewer parameters). Super Tiny Language Models (arXiv 2405.14159) proposes a byte-level embedder pooled at BPE token boundaries for 10-100M models, with no result reported for that component. What remains untested is the byte-position codec at 20M, scored on chat-name recall.
- **L8 (surface-form-robust pre-tokenization for recalling names): partially tried.** Sun et al. ("Tokenization Consistency Matters for Generative Models on Extractive NLP Tasks", Findings of EMNLP 2023, arXiv 2212.09912) identify the same mechanism: a span copied from the input gets a different BPE sequence because of a preceding space. It measurably hurts generative extractive QA (BART-base) and causes hallucination, and training with consistent tokenization gives "+1.7 F1" on average. StochasTok (arXiv 2506.01687, 2025) is a related training-time fix (random token splitting) for subword-level understanding. Untested: chat-name recall at 20M.
- **L11 (chat-phrase superword tokens added to a small vocabulary): partially tried.** Multi-Word Tokenization (Gee et al., EMNLP 2023, arXiv 2402.09949) adds frequent multi-word expressions as single tokens and reports better quality at a fixed sequence budget plus speedups (encoder models). SuperBPE and Ferrando cover the rest of the design space. Untested: a few hundred chat phrases on an 8k decoder, scored on bits per byte.
- **L12 (Planck vocabulary as a subset of the teacher's vocabulary, to keep logit distillation open): partially tried.** Building a small vocabulary as a subset of a larger model's vocabulary, to stay compatible with it, is established. PanGu-pi Pro derives a compact tokenizer from its parent's 100k and then inherits parameters (arXiv 2402.02791). Nozaki et al. (COLING 2025 Industry) cut Llama-3's 128k to 8k by merge-order truncation. FR-Spec (arXiv 2502.14856) and VocabTrim (arXiv 2506.22694) restrict a draft model's head to a frequency-ranked subset of the target vocabulary. Also relevant: "Breaking the Token Ceiling" (arXiv 2609.12303, Meta, Sep 2026) converts teacher token logits *exactly* into byte logits ("End-Of-Token"), a tokenizer-independent route to logit distillation that could make the subset constraint unnecessary (measured at about 1B). Untested: the compression cost of a Gemma-subset 8k against a free in-domain 8k on Planck chat.
- **L7 (rare-name recall vs vocabulary size, stratified by token count):** I found no direct prior study in a limited search, so "untested" stands. Related: Kharitonov, Baroni and Hupkes (arXiv 2110.02782) show that a larger BPE vocabulary makes Transformers memorize *training data* more easily "even when we control for the number of learned parameters". That is a different mechanism from in-context copying, but it belongs in the L7 write-up.

## Omissions

1. **SpaceByte** (Slagle, NeurIPS 2024, arXiv 2404.14408). This is compute-controlled byte-vs-subword evidence at 1e18-1e19 FLOPs (models of about 130M-450M), much closer to Planck's compute than BLT or H-Net. It shows (a) plain byte Transformers 15-29% worse than a SentencePiece model at 1e19 and needing about 10x FLOPs, and (b) space-boundary hierarchical bytes matching subword. It corrects the "parity only at 400M-8B" line and is the right comparison arm if L14 (byte-level control) is run. It is still 1-2 orders above Planck's compute.
2. **Compute Optimal Tokenization App. F.5 / Figure 25 and its comparison with Tao** (same paper, already cited). These are the byte-level curve and the authors' own statement that their T* does not apply when vocabulary cost is coupled to compression (see rows 4-5).
3. **Breaking the Token Ceiling** (Marathe, Pagnoni, Limisiewicz et al., arXiv 2609.12303, Sep 2026). Distillation into byte vs token students at about 1B, up to 1T bytes. Token models win in the low-FLOP regime and byte models overtake later. It also gives an exact token-to-byte logit conversion. This is relevant to open question 8 and to L12. Scale: about 1B only.
4. **Same-vocabulary logit KD at 140M** (MobileLLM-R1, arXiv 2509.24945). It bears on open question 8: logit KD into a sub-200M student from an 8B teacher has been done, but only by adopting the teacher's 128k vocabulary (53% of parameters).
5. **Vocabulary trimming and subsetting**: Nozaki et al. COLING 2025 Industry (https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-industry.64.pdf), FR-Spec 2502.14856, VocabTrim 2506.22694, VocabTailor 2508.15229. These are prior art for L12 and for "truncate a merge list" (L3).
6. **Tokenization consistency** (Sun et al., arXiv 2212.09912). Prior art and a ready-made fix for L8.
7. **Kharitonov et al. 2021** (arXiv 2110.02782). Vocabulary size vs memorization at controlled parameter count, for L7.
8. **Takase et al.** (Findings of ACL 2025, arXiv 2406.16508). A 680M body (embeddings not counted) trained on 1T tokens with vocabularies of 5k-500k: larger is better. It is a counterweight to cite, though it does not transfer to a fixed 20M total.
9. **Multi-Word Tokenization** (arXiv 2402.09949) and **StochasTok** (arXiv 2506.01687), for L11 and L10 respectively.
10. Minor: "Lifecycle-Optimal Tokenization" (arXiv 2608.11361, Aug 2026) reports bits per byte flat within 2% across its optimal vocabulary range at 1.3-2.3B, and recommends about 32k for on-device batch-1 serving. Large-scale, low relevance.

## What to carry forward

- Byte-level penalty for the writeup: about 13% bits per byte at 1e19 for V=256 (Figure 25, approximate), 12-18% for a plain byte Transformer at 1e19 (SpaceByte Table 1), larger below. Do not cite 19% as the byte number.
- Drop the "reach 4.3-4.8 bytes/token via in-domain training" logic. The realistic in-domain 8k target on chat is about 3.8-4.0 bytes/token. The best available bound on what that costs, with embeddings free, is about 1-2% bits per byte (Limisiewicz Table 2), far less than the cost of losing 9-14 layers.
- Center the 20M vocabulary on the 302M-anchored 6.2k (law 8.0k as the upper end). The 4k-vs-8k call stays open, as the report says.
- Give the 4k and 32k arms about 0.5M extra parameters (wider FFN) so every arm is within ±1% of 20.0M.
- If a byte-level control is run for the writeup, run a SpaceByte-style arm, not only a plain byte Transformer. Otherwise the result only restates the known plain-byte penalty.
