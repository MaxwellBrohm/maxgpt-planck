# Follow-up track: a "TinyStories for conversation" dataset

Research date 2026-09-23. Builds on the verified first-run lanes (`research/lanes/data.md` + `data.verify.md`, `eval.md`, `posttrain.md`, `compute.md` + verify files) and treats `research/lanes/probe.md` as an unaudited hint only. Where a first-run fact-check corrected a claim, the corrected version is used. Every number has a source URL or says "derived" (my arithmetic from named sourced inputs) or "unsourced". Scale is stated for every result; **[>=1B only]** flags evidence that exists only at 1B parameters or more. No model was loaded or run for this report.

---

## Bottom line

1. The pieces exist but the dataset does not. Tiny-register chat corpora exist (TinyChat: 1M GPT-4o-mini small-talk conversations in BASIC English; TinyDialogues: about 130k GPT-4 child-directed dialogues), and multi-turn-skill synthesis exists for 7B+ models (Parrot, ConsistentChat, TurnWise, SmolTalk2's Multi-Turn IF), but I found no dataset that combines a register a 10-150M model can learn with dense, machine-verified cross-turn skills (recall at controlled distance, reference, correction, persistence, topic return, speaker binding, abstention), and no report of such data being trained into a sub-100M model and measured on cross-turn memory. What is new is that combination plus the measurement at tiny scale, not any single component.
2. The TinyStories lesson needs a 2025 update: Lee and Berg-Kirkpatrick (COLM 2025, 262K-33M non-embedding params, 10B training tokens) found that "statistical simplicity, not developmental readability" predicts learnability; adult-vocabulary synthetic stories were learned as fast or faster than child-level ones. So the design rule is: keep the distribution statistically narrow (few conversation shapes, a controlled word list, no long repeated spans) and force diversity through random skeleton features, not "talk like a 3-year-old".
3. The core design is skeleton, render, verify: a program samples the conversation's facts, skill events, distances, persona and required words; a teacher renders it into short natural turns; a checker verifies every planted fact, every answer, every constraint, and discards failures with a reason code. In this "script mode" the teacher never has to remember anything, so its multi-turn reputation matters much less than its instruction following, register control and accepted-tokens-per-hour.
4. Gemma 4 26B-A4B checks out on license (Apache 2.0) and has a clean thinking-off switch (no `<|think|>` / `enable_thinking=False`; the 26B, 31B and 12B still emit an empty thought block that must be stripped), but all its published benchmarks are thinking-mode, and on a 24 GB M5 it is a poor fit for batched generation: about 14-15 GB of 4-bit weights under a roughly 75% default Metal limit leaves little KV room, and at batch 16-32 a 128-expert top-8 MoE touches 64-87% of its experts every step, erasing most of the "3.8B active" advantage (derived). Gemma 4 12B (dense, Apache 2.0, IFBench 74.0 vs 72.0 for the 26B, thinking mode) is the likelier Mac workhorse; the choice should be made by a 200-skeleton yield-per-hour pilot, not by reputation.
5. Runtime: MLX (mlx-lm BatchGenerator, or a continuous-batching server such as vllm-mlx) over llama.cpp for batched generation. The only measured Gemma 4 26B-A4B MLX number is 113 tok/s single-stream on an M5 Max (614 GB/s); scaled to the M5's 153 GB/s that is about 28 tok/s (derived). The Titans cannot run Gemma 4 under vLLM with driver 470 (Gemma 4 support starts at vLLM 0.19.0; the last CUDA 11.8 wheel is 0.10.1.1), and Gemma has a real fp16 overflow history (Gemma 3 activations up to about 800,000 vs the fp16 max of 65,504); Gemma 4 added a per-block scale "to enable stable inference in fp16", but only the vision tower has a reported fp16 overflow so far, and nothing is measured on Turing.
6. Falcon-H1-Tiny's anti-curriculum result, as corrected by the first-run fact-check, is an instruction-following result (IFEval), not a chat or multi-turn one. Its practical implication for this dataset is size and format: if the chat data is to be 25%+ of pretraining from step 0 with spaced repetition, it must be tens to hundreds of millions of unique tokens (not an SFT-sized set), use the final chat template from the first step, and sit next to at least 20% general text. Whether from-step-0 beats a late phase for multi-turn skill is untested and is ledger idea L6.
7. Budget: for the 20M headline model, target about 100M unique tokens of verified conversation (roughly 400-500k conversations of about 200-250 tokens), with a 20-30M-token pilot first. At an estimated 100-300 accepted tok/s on the M5 that is roughly 4-12 Mac-days for the full set and 1-3 days for the pilot (derived), and it competes with training for the same machine.

**Verdict.** Three families exist and none covers the target:
- Tiny-register chat (TinyChat, TinyDialogues, TinyChat-1776K) has the register but no memory demand: in the TinyChat rows I sampled (3 via the datasets-server rows API, plus the card example), every user turn is self-contained small talk, and TinyDialogues is caregiver speech. No checks, no planted facts.
- Multi-turn-skill data (Parrot, ConsistentChat, TurnWise, SmolTalk2 Multi-Turn IF, TopicGuidedChat) has the skills in big-model register (hundreds of tokens per turn) and was only tested at 7B+.
- Programmatic dialogue (bAbI) is fully verifiable but narrow and unnatural.

The novel part is: (a) a skeleton that densely schedules cross-turn skill events at controlled distances, (b) rendering in a statistically narrow register a 10-150M model can learn, (c) programmatic verification of every event with teacher-failure discard and per-skill yield accounting, (d) knowledge-free and lookup variants, and (e) a controlled measurement of whether such data moves cross-turn skill at 5-150M. Confidence that nothing like this exists is medium: absence claims are only as good as the searches (queries listed in section 7).

### 2. What made TinyStories learnable, and what 2025 work changed

- **Vocabulary plus random requirements.** About 1,500 basic words split into nouns, verbs and adjectives; each prompt requires one random verb, noun and adjective, plus a random subset of features (dialogue, plot twist, bad ending, moral value, foreshadowing, conflict). The authors state that prompting, "even if the temperature of generation is set to a high value, will still produce a very repetitive dataset" ([arXiv 2305.07759](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.07759), sec. 2). Diversity came from the random constraints, not from sampling temperature. SimpleStories went further with parameterized prompts at several abstraction levels and reports better sample efficiency ([arXiv 2504.09184](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09184)); TinyChat copied the recipe for chat with random words, tone, opening type and ending type ([post](https://nikhilr.io/posts/TinyChat15M/)).
- **Depth matters for instruction following.** In TinyStories, 1-layer models "struggle quite substantially with following instructions", 2 layers are enough "to a certain extent", and instruction following depends more on depth than plot coherence does, which depends more on width (sec. 3.1). Measured at 1M-80M params. Cross-turn recall is an instruction-following-like global-attention skill, so Planck's 10-20M shapes should not trade layers for width.
- **Readability is not the lever; statistical simplicity is.** Lee and Berg-Kirkpatrick rebuilt TinyStories with Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (LlamaTales-Jr) and a GRE-vocabulary version (LlamaTales-GRE), about 1B tokens each, and trained 262K to 33M non-embedding-param models for 10B tokens (10 epochs). Their conclusion: "statistical simplicity, not developmental readability, is the more reliable predictor of learnability in SLMs"; GRE-level models passed an LLM-judged coherence of 85 after the first epoch. Unique 8-grams: TinyStories 5.02e8, LlamaTales-Jr 6.48e8, LlamaTales-GRE 6.88e8, FineWeb 9.43e8 ([arXiv 2510.13915](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13915), [html](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13915v1)). Two design consequences: an open-weight 8B teacher was enough to reproduce TinyStories-quality data, and a child-level word list is not required; what is required is a narrow set of conversation shapes and phrasings.
- **Simplifying real text is not enough at 1-14M.** TinyHelen's 2,000-word Leaner sets (71M + 7M tokens) still gave instruction following rated poor at 14M ([arXiv 2501.00522](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00522)). Small token budgets plus single-turn instruct data did not produce a conversational model.

**Carry-over to chat.** Keep: a controlled word list, random required words, random features, short units. Change: the "features" become conversation skill events with parameters (which skill, at what distance, with which distractors), and the random requirements also cover persona, topic, user style and slot values. Add what stories never needed: machine verification, because a chat skill is either executed correctly or not.

### 3. Dataset design

#### 3.1 Unit of generation: skeleton, render, verify

1. **Skeleton (program).** Sample: assistant persona (system prompt name, style rules), user persona (name, age band, 2-4 personal facts drawn from slot pools, some nonce values), topic path (1-3 everyday topics from a list of a few hundred), 2-4 skill events with parameters (plant turn, query turn, distance, distractor values, correction or not), user style (terse, chatty, typo-prone), opening and closing type, 3 required words from the word list, and turn count (4-8 user turns).
2. **Render (teacher).** The teacher gets the skeleton and writes the whole conversation in one generation ("script mode"), with hard limits: user turns at most about 25 words, assistant turns about 5-30 words, plain sentences, no lists unless the skeleton asks.
3. **Verify (program).** Checks in section 3.6; failures are discarded with a reason code, and a skeleton gets at most one retry with a new seed.

Why script mode for the bulk: one generation per conversation (no re-prefill of the history at every turn), and the teacher cannot fail a recall because it is told the answer. The check then verifies *rendering fidelity*: that the fact was planted where scheduled, that it did not leak into the turns between plant and query, that the user's question refers back instead of restating, and that the answer is right and unambiguous.

A smaller **live mode** subset is still worth generating: a simulator writes user turns from the skeleton, the assistant model answers turn by turn seeing only the conversation, and the checker verifies the assistant's recall. This is the original "plant a fact, auto-check the teacher's later answer" plan. It costs more (per-turn prefill, lower yield), but its assistant turns are the teacher's natural answers, and its discard rate is itself a measurement of teacher multi-turn reliability. Cross-model pairing (different simulator and assistant models) gave more coherent dialogues than self-chat in DeepDialogue (acceptance 0.65 vs 0.54, 4B-72B generators, [arXiv 2505.19978](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19978), per data.verify).

Parrot's diagnosis applies to both modes: simulated users write "information-complete" queries ([arXiv 2310.07301](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07301)). The skeleton fixes this by construction: query turns are scheduled to use referring expressions ("that", "the second one", "my sister's") and the checker rejects any query turn that restates the value.

#### 3.2 Skill families

Each family maps to an evaluation family from the eval lane's MTB-150 battery (`research/lanes/eval.md`, section D2) and to a failure class seen in the unaudited probe (`research/lanes/probe.md`, bottom line items 3-7: deflection is the largest failure class; corrections fail at every size up to 0.6B; speaker binding and "answering as the user" occur; own-reply copying occurs). Training templates and slot pools must be disjoint from the eval's sealed split.

| # | Skill | Skeleton event | Programmatic check | Eval family | Probe failure targeted |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1 | Fact recall at distance | plant a user fact at turn k, query at k+d, d from 1 to 8, with same-type distractor values elsewhere | answer contains the value (normalized), contains no other in-conversation candidate; value absent from turns k+1..k+d-1; query does not contain it | F1, LP-Recall | wrong value, deflection |
| S2 | Reference, follow-up, list state | user lists items, adds or removes some, then "what was the second one?", "move the last one to the top" | exact item or ordered-list match | F3, LP-Coref | wrong index, copying |
| S3 | Correction and update | user changes a fact ("actually it's 4 pm"); or assistant errs and the user corrects it | new value present, stale value absent in every later answer | F2, F5, LP-Update | stale value preferred (fails at every size in the probe) |
| S4 | Instruction persistence and override | a rule in the system prompt or turn 1 ("answer in under 15 words", "end with a question", "call me Max"); sometimes replaced later | an IFEval-style verifier on every later assistant turn, plus an on-topic gate so an empty or generic reply cannot pass (eval lane D2) | F4, F5 | rule dropped after turn 1 |
| S5 | Topic return | digression of n turns, then "anyway, back to your trip" | the answer names an entity from the original topic and none from the digression | new (not in MTB-150; add it) | drift |
| S6 | Speaker and perspective binding | "my sister Ana" vs "my brother Leo"; assistant persona facts in the system prompt vs user facts | the right person is bound; the assistant says "your dog", never "my dog", for user facts; no "User:" line; no user fact claimed as the assistant's | F7, LP-Persona | speaker binding, answering as the user |
| S7 | Abstain when not given, and do not deflect when given | ask about a fact never stated; separately, ask about a stated fact | abstain items: a hedge pattern and no candidate value; given items: the answer uses the fact and matches no deflection pattern ("I don't have access to personal information", "as an AI I can't know") | F2 abstain | deflection (largest class in the probe) |
| S8 | Social basics | greeting, thanks, "who are you", goodbye | identity string matches the system prompt, length bounds, no topic injection | F8 | "Hi" failures (SmolTalk everyday-conversations precedent) |
| S9 | Lookup (lookup variant only) | a world-knowledge question the conversation cannot answer | a tool call is issued if and only if needed; the answer copies the value from the result; "no result" is reported as such; stated facts are never looked up | new | invented facts |

Skill density: 2-4 events per conversation, so that most assistant turns either set up or pay off an event. Filler turns are ordinary small talk that serve as distractors.

Loss masking: when the skeleton includes a deliberately wrong assistant turn (S3's "assistant errs, user corrects" case), mask that turn's loss so the model learns to accept corrections without learning to make the error.

#### 3.3 TinyStories-style diversity levers for chat

- Random required words: 3 per conversation from the word list (one noun, one verb, one adjective), as in TinyStories and TinyDialogues.
- Random features: skill events, distances, turn count, user style, emotional tone, opening type (greeting, question, statement), closing type (goodbye, open, abrupt), topic path, assistant persona style.
- Slot pools: names (common and nonce), ages, pets, places, times, numbers, foods, colors, plus nonce strings. Nonce values force copying from the context rather than guessing a frequent value, the same logic as entity substitution in knowledge-conflict training (Longpre et al., EMNLP 2021: substitution-augmented training gave 4-7% out-of-distribution gains and less hallucination; QA reader models, sizes not re-checked, [arXiv 2109.05052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.05052)).
- Temperature is a secondary lever (TinyStories quote above). Use the teacher's recommended sampling (Gemma 4: temperature 1.0, top_p 0.95, top_k 64, [card](https://huggingface.co/google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it)) and let the skeleton supply the diversity.

#### 3.4 Register and vocabulary

- Controlled word list for connective text (for example 2,000-5,000 common words; TinyHelen used 2,000, TinyStories about 1,500, TinyChat BASIC English's 850), with slot values exempt. Check the out-of-list rate per conversation.
- Short turns: user at most about 25 words, assistant about 5-30 words. For comparison: UltraChat averages 309 tokens per utterance, SODA 22.5 (data lane, [arXiv 2305.14233](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14233)); SODA's register is the right order of magnitude.
- Low short-n-gram diversity is acceptable, and per Readability ≠ Learnability probably helpful. High long-n-gram diversity is required: no assistant sentence or 8-gram repeated across many conversations. Repeated training text drives degenerate loops, including after instruction tuning ([arXiv 2310.10226](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10226)), and MaxGPT-2/3 showed such loops.
- Tension to test, not assume: a restricted vocabulary helps learnability but real users type any word. The final eval will contain words outside any list. Ledger idea L4 measures the knee.

#### 3.5 Knowledge-free vs knowledge-with-lookup variants

- **Knowledge-free core (default for 10-60M).** Facts come only from the conversation or the system prompt, plus "tiny world" commonsense at TinyStories level (dogs bark, rain is wet). Any other factual question is answered with an abstention and an offer ("I don't know that. If you tell me, I'll remember it."). Rationale: knowledge costs about 2 bits per parameter at best (Physics of LMs 3.3, [arXiv 2404.05405](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05405), GPT-2-family models on synthetic biographies), and even Qwen2.5-0.5B scores TriviaQA 4.3, the same floor as SmolLM2-135M (data.verify), so a small model's facts are noise either way.
- **Lookup variant (for 60-150M, or as a 20-30% slice).** Add a tool format, for example `<lookup>query</lookup>` followed by `<result>...</result>`. Results come from a program-built fact table, mostly fictional entities with random attribute values (so the only way to answer is to read), plus some real facts for realism, plus empty results. Checks: a lookup is issued if and only if the answer is not already in the conversation; the answer copies the result value; empty results are reported. Pleias-RAG-350M and 1B show small models can be trained to read sources (vendor, [arXiv 2504.18225](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.18225), claim made for the 350M and 1B jointly per data.verify).
- Counterfactual lookup results (a result that contradicts common knowledge) are the cleanest test that the model reads rather than recalls; see L15.

#### 3.6 Verification and teacher-failure discard

Every conversation must pass all of these; each failure gets a reason code.

1. Structure: the scheduled number of turns, alternating roles, no empty turns, no role labels inside turn text, no thought-channel tags left over, no markdown unless scheduled.
2. Length bounds per turn.
3. Vocabulary: out-of-list rate at most a set threshold (for example 2%), slot values excluded.
4. Planting: each planted value appears in its plant turn (normalized: case, number words vs digits).
5. Distance integrity: the planted value is absent between plant and query, except scheduled mentions.
6. Query form: the query turn does not contain the value and, for S2/S6, contains a referring expression from the scheduled list.
7. Answer correctness: the answer turn contains the correct value and none of the distractor candidates (other same-type values in the conversation, the stale value after a correction).
8. Perspective: user facts appear with second-person forms in assistant turns.
9. Persistence: an IFEval-style verifier on every assistant turn after a rule, plus the on-topic gate.
10. Abstention: hedge pattern and no candidate value on abstain items; no deflection pattern on given items.
11. Degeneracy: no 4-gram repeated 3 or more times within a turn (the probe's metric), consecutive-repetition coverage below 0.25 (the Daimax metric cited in the eval lane), and no assistant turn copying 50% or more of the previous one.
12. Language and safety: English only, a simple safety filter (SODA also filtered harmful content).
13. Not a gate: a second model judges naturalness on a 1-2% sample, to catch checks that pass stilted text.

Mutation-test the checker before trusting it (Max's standing rule, and the eval lane's own requirement): for every check, feed it an empty turn, a wrong in-pool value, a "shotgun" answer that lists every candidate, and an echo of the question, and confirm each fails.

Yield accounting: log acceptance per skill family, distance and teacher. Discarding silently under-represents whatever the teacher finds hardest (likely corrections and long distances), so re-weight skeleton sampling toward low-yield cells until the accepted set matches the target mixture.

#### 3.7 Diversity and dedup measurement (run before any training)

- Exact dedup on normalized text; near-duplicate removal with MinHash-LSH on word 5-grams (Jaccard threshold around 0.8 is common practice; the threshold is a choice, unsourced here).
- Long-span caps: count corpus frequency of every assistant sentence and every 8-gram; cap any non-template 8-gram (for example at 0.1% of conversations).
- Short-span statistics as learnability indicators: distinct-1/2/3 and unique 3-grams per million tokens (Lee and Berg-Kirkpatrick report learnability tracking n-gram diversity).
- Corpus-level homogeneity: gzip compression ratio and ROUGE-L homogenization. Shaib et al. found cheap compression scores capture much of what slow n-gram homogeneity scores measure, and released a package ([arXiv 2403.00553](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.00553), [code](https://github.com/cshaib/diversity)).
- Coverage matrix: accepted counts per (skill x distance x topic x user style x opening type), with a minimum per cell.
- Mode-collapse check: embed the first user turn of every conversation and cluster; a few giant clusters mean the openings collapsed (Cosmopedia needed explicit seeding to get under 1% duplicates, data lane).
- Contamination: n-gram overlap between training data and the eval's sealed split must be near zero; ideally render the sealed eval with a different teacher than the training data.

#### 3.8 Target size in tokens for a 20M model

Derived, with the inputs named:
- Training budget: Chinchilla-style 20 tokens/param is 0.4B tokens; tiny-story models have been trained to 10B tokens at up to 33M non-embedding params ([arXiv 2510.13915](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13915)). The Mac measured 10.6-11.8k tok/s on an 18.5M proxy with a 49k vocabulary (compute lane; a small-vocabulary 20M model should be somewhat faster, unmeasured), so about 1B tokens per Mac-day. A 2-4B-token run (100-200 tokens/param) costs 2-4 Mac-days: affordable for the headline model, not for ablations.
- Chat share: 25-50% of those tokens (see section 5) is 0.5-2B chat token-passes.
- Repetition: up to 4 epochs is nearly as good as unique data and value decays by 1/e around 16 epochs (Muennighoff et al., 10M-9B params, [arXiv 2305.16264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.16264)); spaced repetition of 100+ passes worked for SFT-type data at 90-100M (TII, one group, data.verify).
- Result: about 50-200M unique chat tokens; aim for about 100M (400-500k conversations at 200-250 tokens) for the 20M model, and 0.3-1B unique for a 150M model where the chat set is a smaller share of a larger run. For comparison, TinyStories is roughly 0.4B tokens and TinyChat roughly 0.18B (derived above).
- Pilot: 20-30M tokens (100-150k conversations), enough for 5-10M-param ablations at 100-300M training tokens with a few epochs.
- Generation cost: at an estimated 100-300 accepted tok/s on the M5 (section 4.4), 100M tokens is about 4-12 Mac-days and the pilot 1-3 days, plus prompt overhead. The same Mac trains the models, so generation and ablations have to be scheduled around each other.

### 4. The teacher

#### 4.1 Gemma 4 26B-A4B: verified facts

- License: Apache 2.0 on the model card and in Hugging Face metadata ([card](https://huggingface.co/google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it), [Google blog, Apr 2 2026](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/gemma-4/)). This corrects the first-run data lane's "Gemma uses its own license", which was true of Gemma 3, not Gemma 4.
- Release: Mar 31 2026 (E2B, E4B, 26B A4B, 31B); MTP drafters Apr 16; 12B Unified Jun 3 ([release notes](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/docs/releases)).
- Architecture (from [config.json](https://huggingface.co/google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it/raw/main/config.json)): 30 layers, hidden 2816, 128 experts with top-8 routing (expert FFN width 704) plus a dense FFN (width 2112) per layer, 25 sliding-window layers (window 1,024) and 5 global layers, vocab 262,144, tied embeddings, bf16. Derived parameter split: experts about 22.8B, attention about 1.1B, dense FFN about 0.5B, embeddings about 0.74B, which reproduces the card's 25.2B total and 3.8B active.
- Thinking off: thinking is enabled by putting `<|think|>` at the start of the system turn and disabled by leaving it out, or with `enable_thinking=False` in `apply_chat_template`. "For all models except for the E2B and E4B variants, if thinking is disabled, the model will still generate the tags but with an empty thought block", so the pipeline must strip the empty `<|channel>thought ... <channel|>` block. In multi-turn use, "the historical model output should only include the final response" ([card](https://huggingface.co/google/gemma-4-26B-A4B-it); template in [Gemma 4 report Table 11](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02770)). Gemma 4 has a native `system` role, unlike Gemma 3.
- Published quality: every instruction-following number is thinking-mode ("All models are in thinking mode unless explicitly stated"): IFEval 98.5, IFBench 72.0 for 26B-A4B; 97.2 and 74.0 for the 12B; 98.9 and 76.0 for the 31B ([report Table 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02770)). Human-preference chat: Arena Text Elo 1438 (rank 61 overall) for 26B-A4B and 1451 for 31B, vs 1366 for Gemma 3 27B, as of Jun 19 2026 (report Table 4); Google's launch post called the 26B the #6 open model. I found no published non-thinking or multi-turn-specific score (MultiChallenge, Multi-IF) for any Gemma 4 model.

**Does the teacher's multi-turn reputation matter?** Less than it appears. In script mode the teacher is told the facts, the distances and the answers; what matters is (1) following a structured spec exactly, (2) writing short plain turns without drifting into assistant boilerplate, (3) naturalness, and (4) accepted tokens per hour. Reputation matters more for the live-mode subset. None of these is published for any candidate, so the decision should come from a pilot: 200 identical skeletons per teacher, measure yield by reason code, accepted tokens per hour, diversity statistics, and a blind naturalness read of 30 conversations each (L12). Also note that DeepDialogue found 4-8B generators losing coherence beyond about 6 turns in *self-chat* (data.verify), which is a live-mode concern, not a script-mode one.

#### 4.3 Runtime on the M5 with 24 GB

- **Memory.** Metal's default working-set limit is reported at roughly 75% of unified memory, about 16-18 GB on a 24 GB machine (third-party measurements, not an Apple spec; query `recommendedMaxWorkingSetSize`) ([ModelPiper](https://modelpiper.com/blog/iogpu-wired-limit-mb-mac), [Pickuma](https://pickuma.com/for-dev/running-local-llms-m4-mac-24gb/)). Raising `iogpu.wired_limit_mb` needs sudo and is a system setting Max would have to choose. Gemma 4 26B-A4B at 4-bit needs about 14-15 GB of weights (derived at 4.5 bits per weight; 17.1 GB measured at 4k context with MLX on an M5 Max, [Incept5](https://github.com/Incept5/gemma4-benchmark)), which leaves roughly 1-3 GB for KV cache and other processes under the default limit. The Mac is also in use by another run.
- **KV per conversation (derived from the configs, bf16 cache).** About 215 KB per token for 26B-A4B and about 330 KB per token for the 12B (sliding layers dominate; they are capped at the 1,024-token window). A 1k-token render therefore holds about 0.2-0.35 GB, so 26B-A4B fits only a handful of concurrent sequences; the 12B fits 16-24 in about 6-8 GB.
- **MoE loses most of its advantage under batching (derived).** With 128 experts and top-8 routing, the expected fraction of experts touched per layer per step is 1-(120/128)^B: 6% at batch 1, 40% at 8, 64% at 16, 87% at 32, 98% at 64. At 4.5 bits per weight, 26B-A4B reads about 2.1 GB per step at batch 1, 9.6 GB at 16, 12.6 GB at 32. Dense 12B reads about 6.7 GB per step at any batch. Tokens delivered per GB read: batch 1, 26B-A4B 3.1x better; batch 16, 12B 1.4x better; batch 32, 12B 1.9x better. Decode on this Mac is bandwidth-bound up to a batch where compute takes over (this Mac measured 12.5-14 TFLOPS on a large bf16 GEMM via MPS, compute lane), which caps the 12B at very roughly 400 tok/s and 26B-A4B at very roughly 250 tok/s at batch 32 as upper bounds. Realistic sustained rates are likely half of those or less, and 26B-A4B cannot reach batch 32 in memory anyway. These are rooflines, not measurements.
- **Measured anchors.** Gemma 4 26B-A4B 4-bit in MLX: 113 tok/s single-stream decode at 4k context, 30 tok/s at 256k, M5 Max ([Incept5](https://github.com/Incept5/gemma4-benchmark)). The M5 Max has up to 614 GB/s ([Apple](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2026/03/apple-debuts-m5-pro-and-m5-max-to-supercharge-the-most-demanding-pro-workflows/)) vs 153 GB/s for the M5 (data lane), so about 28 tok/s single-stream on this Mac at equal efficiency (derived). I found no published base-M5 measurement for any candidate teacher.
- **MLX vs llama.cpp.** vllm-mlx on an M4 Max measured batching gains of 3.7x (Qwen3-0.6B) and 2.6x (Qwen3-8B) at 16 concurrent requests ([arXiv 2601.19139](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.19139), data lane). A five-framework study on an M2 Ultra found "MLX achieves the highest sustained generation throughput" while llama.cpp is "highly efficient for lightweight single-stream use" ([arXiv 2511.05502](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.05502)). An open llama.cpp issue filed today reports Metal decode throughput collapsing when long sequences are batched with an MoE model (M3 Ultra, 16k-32k contexts; two separate processes 4.6x faster than one batched process) ([issue #29335](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/29335)); short renders may not hit it, but it argues against llama.cpp slots for this job. mlx-community publishes 4-bit Gemma 4 conversions ([example](https://huggingface.co/mlx-community/gemma-4-26b-a4b-it-4bit)); vllm-metal (v0.28, Sep 22 2026) now offers concurrent serving and an NAX attention kernel on M5 ([vLLM blog](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-22-vllm-metal-v0-28-0)). Recommendation: mlx-lm batch generation in script mode, or a continuous-batching MLX server with prefix caching for live mode (the shared system prompt and skeleton instructions are a long common prefix).

#### 4.4 Gemma on the Turing Titans (fp16)

- **Software block first.** vLLM added Gemma 4 in v0.19.0 (Apr 3 2026) ([vLLM blog](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-04-02-gemma4)); the last CUDA 11.8 vLLM wheel is v0.10.1.1 and driver 470 caps the box at CUDA 11.x (compute.verify). So there is no vLLM path to Gemma 4 on the Titans without an IT driver upgrade. llama.cpp documents CUDA 11.7 builds ([build docs](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/docs/build.md)), but a current Gemma 4 build under CUDA 11 on sm_75 is unverified. The Titans are busy with Ultra until about December regardless.
- **fp16 overflow history.** Gemma 3 activations "can be 800,000", far above fp16's 65,504, causing inf/NaN on T4-class and V100 GPUs; Unsloth's fix kept activations in higher precision ([Daniel Han](https://x.com/danielhanchen/status/1902396261875249346), [Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/blog/gemma3)). Gemma 4's report says: "to enable stable inference in fp16, we introduce a scalar scale at each block in order to bound the activation ranges to fit fp16" (sec. 2.5, [arXiv 2607.02770](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.02770)); the paragraph sits in the quantization section, and whether it covers the bf16 release checkpoints is not stated. The one reported Gemma 4 fp16 overflow is the vision tower (standardization bias about 53,760; text-only requests "work fine"), measured on an RTX 5090 with vLLM 0.19 ([issue #40290](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/40290)). Nothing is published for the Gemma 4 text path on Turing. If the Titans are ever used, run 100 prompts in fp16 and in fp32, check for inf/NaN and compare logits before generating anything.
- The RTX 5070 (Blackwell) runs bf16 natively, so the overflow question does not arise there.

### 5. What Falcon-H1-Tiny's anti-curriculum implies for this dataset

What the evidence says, with the first-run corrections applied: at 90M and 800B tokens, 25% SFT-format data in all of pretraining plus DPO gave IFEval 66.08 vs 53.47 for pretrain-then-SFT plus DPO, while the curriculum model was slightly better on 2-turn MT-Bench (4.4 vs 4.33) and AlpacaEval (10.44 vs 9.43). The 25% ratio came from 100 GT ablations in which 25% and 50% tied for instruction following; the 100M multilingual variant saw no clear boost ([blog](https://tiiuae-tiny-h1-blogpost.hf.space/), data.verify row 2). It says nothing about cross-turn memory.

Implications:
1. **Size.** A share of 25% or more of a 2-4B-token run with spaced repetition needs tens to hundreds of millions of unique chat tokens, which is where the 100M target in section 3.8 comes from. An SFT-sized set (Max's current 108k rows) would be repeated hundreds of times.
2. **Format from step 0.** The final chat template, role tokens, and the lookup tool format must be in the data from the first step, identical in any later stage.
3. **Keep general text.** TII kept about 20% web; their memorization-window study found 6% low-quality web beat pure high-quality repetition (data lane). For Planck at 10-20M the natural companion is simple narrative text (TinyStories, SimpleStories or an open-teacher LlamaTales-style set) plus some real conversational text.
4. **Loss on user turns.** In pretraining mode the model is trained to predict user turns too; in SFT mode it is not. That could help comprehension or feed the "answers as the user" failure. It is a cheap, untested switch (L8).
5. **For a knowledge-free chat model the base model is the chat model.** There is no knowledge-heavy base to protect, so the usual argument for keeping pretraining "pure" is weak at 10-60M. At 150M, where some general knowledge and language breadth are wanted, the chat set becomes one ingredient among several (data lane recipe: 15-25% chat-format, 5-10% targeted multi-turn).
6. **Test it on the right metric.** The only controlled evidence is IFEval. Run the three-arm comparison (from step 0, late phase only, pretrain then SFT) on the multi-turn probes (L6).

## What this means for Planck at 10M-150M

- **10-20M (headline small point).** The verified chat set, knowledge-free, can be 25-75% of all training tokens, with simple narrative text as the rest; train a small tokenizer (a few thousand tokens) on this mix so the embedding table stays around 2-3M params. Keep enough depth for global-attention skills (TinyStories: instruction following depends on layers). Unique chat data about 100M tokens; total training 2-4B tokens. Evaluate with the eval lane's likelihood probes during training and the generative families at the end.
- **60M.** Same skill families, broader word list, add the lookup variant as a slice, and start measuring against Falcon-H1-Tiny-90M-Instruct on the eval battery (the model to beat per the eval lane).
- **150M.** The chat set becomes a 10-25% ingredient of a larger mixed corpus with 0.3-1B unique tokens, knowledge-light, with the lookup variant as the default knowledge path.
- **Across sizes.** Use the same skeleton generator with register parameters (word list size, turn length, distance range) scaled by model size, so the size curve measures the model, not a changing dataset.
- **Counter-evidence to keep in view.** At 151M, ufakzeka-1 reported that multi-turn identity tracking "did not move across any data change we tried" (Turkish, 13.5B tokens, LLM-judged; data.verify omission 3). The probe hint says the 90-600M failures are mostly behavioural (deflection, binding, corrections) rather than raw memory. Both mean the dataset's hypothesis can fail, which is why L1 compares it against an equal-token control with 3 seeds before any large generation run.

## Ledger ideas

Cheapest-test notes: "proxy" means a 5-10M-param model with a small vocabulary trained on the Mac for 100-300M tokens (a few hours per run at the measured 10-12k tok/s class; faster with a small vocabulary, unmeasured), 3 seeds per arm, scored on the eval lane's LP probes (zero-variance likelihood) plus the F1-F8 generative families on a held-out template set. All of these run after the current Mac job finishes, not now.

| # | Hypothesis | Why it could work | Cheapest test on the Mac | Win condition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Skill-dense verified chat data teaches cross-turn skills at 5-20M that equal tokens of TinyChat-style small talk do not | Nobody has put back-reference-dense data into a tiny model; small-talk data gives little gradient for recall (the TinyChat rows sampled here had only self-contained user turns) | Two proxies at equal tokens: (a) 50% verified set + 50% simple narrative, (b) 50% TinyChat-style small talk (render it with the same teacher, since TinyChat itself is CC-BY-NC) + 50% narrative | LP-Recall at distance 4 or more and LP-Update each at least +15 points, seed ranges not overlapping, general-text loss within 2% |
| L2 | Template-only rendering (no LLM) teaches the skills as well as LLM rendering at equal tokens | The skill lives in the skeleton; bAbI-style data is fully verifiable and free | Same skeletons rendered by templates vs by the teacher; train proxies; evaluate on held-out LLM-rendered conversations | If template-only is within 3 points on the probes, spend teacher budget only on naturalness; if the LLM version is 5+ points better on held-out families, keep LLM rendering |
| L3 | Lower short-n-gram diversity (a narrow register) speeds skill learning for chat, as it does for stories | Readability ≠ Learnability found learnability tracks n-gram diversity (262K-33M params) | Render the same skeletons in a controlled-list register and a free register; train proxies; compare learning curves | Controlled register reaches 80% of its final probe score in 70% of the tokens or fewer, with no loss on out-of-list user input |
| L4 | There is a vocabulary-breadth knee: 1.5k vs 5k vs unrestricted connective vocabulary at 20M | Narrow vocabulary helps learnability, but real users type any word | Three proxies at 20M (or 10M) with equal tokens; eval sets with in-list and out-of-list user wording | The smallest list whose out-of-list score is within 5 points of unrestricted |
| L5 | Nonce slot values force copy-from-context and generalize better than real-name values | Entity substitution reduces reliance on memorized values (Longpre 2021) | 0% vs 50% nonce values in slots; test on unseen real names and unseen nonce strings | The nonce-trained model is at least as good on real names and 10+ points better on nonce strings |
| L6 | Chat data from step 0 beats a late phase or pretrain-then-SFT for multi-turn skill, not only IFEval | Falcon-H1-Tiny's IFEval win; mid-training helps in proportion to closeness to the target (70M-1B, arXiv 2510.14865) | Three arms at 8M with equal total tokens: 25% from step 0; same chat tokens only in the last 20%; pretrain then SFT | From-step-0 at least +5 on the multi-turn composite with no more than 1% worse general-text loss |
| L7 | At 10-20M the best chat share is higher than Falcon's 25% because there is no knowledge to protect | The target is chat only; knowledge is offloaded | 25% / 50% / 75% chat share at equal tokens | The knee: the share after which probes stop improving or narrative loss degrades by more than 3% |
| L8 | Masking the loss on user turns during pretraining reduces role leaks and speaker-binding errors without hurting recall | Predicting user turns trains the model to speak as the user; the probe saw "answering as the user" | Masked vs unmasked user-turn loss, same data | Role-leak and wrong-speaker rates at least halved with recall within 2 points (or the reverse result, which is also worth knowing) |
| L9 | Upweighting the loss on skill-bearing answer tokens speeds learning | Answer slots are a tiny fraction of tokens; most loss is spent on filler | Weight x3 on tokens inside answer spans vs x1 | Probe learning curve reaches the x1 final score in 60% of the tokens or fewer, with no fluency or loop-rate regression |
| L10 | Filtering deflection from teacher outputs removes the largest failure class seen in small models | Deflection was 36 of 95 failed user-fact checks in the unaudited probe; it is learned behaviour, not capacity | Train with vs without the deflection filter (keep abstentions only where the fact was never given) | Deflection on given facts below 2% while abstain accuracy on never-given facts stays at or above 90% |
| L11 | Loss-masked assistant errors followed by user corrections teach correction acceptance | Corrections failed at every size up to 0.6B in the probe; data rarely contains them | Add S3 "assistant errs, user corrects" conversations (erroneous turn masked) vs S3 without that case | LP-Update and the probe's correction test at least +15 points |
| L12 | Teacher choice should be decided by accepted tokens per Mac-hour, not reputation | Script mode removes the need for teacher memory; MoE batching arithmetic favors the dense 12B on this Mac | 200 identical skeletons each through Gemma 4 12B, Gemma 4 26B-A4B, Ministral 3 8B, Qwen3.5-9B (thinking off, MLX 4-bit); log yield by reason code, tok/s, diversity stats, blind naturalness read | Pick the highest accepted-tokens-per-hour teacher whose naturalness rating is within 10% of the best |
| L13 | Script-mode rendering produces training data as good as live-mode (turn-by-turn) data at a fraction of the cost | One generation per conversation vs per-turn prefill | Same skeletons in both modes; train proxies on each at equal tokens | Script mode within 3 points on held-out generative families at less than half the Mac-hours per accepted token |
| L14 | A second model rendering user turns (cross-model pairing) raises diversity without hurting skill | DeepDialogue: cross-model beats self-chat in coherence (4B-72B generators); two models have different phrasings | Gemma renders assistant turns and Ministral renders user turns vs Gemma renders both; compare compression ratio, unique 3-grams, and proxy probe scores | Lower compression ratio (more diverse) with probe scores within 2 points |
| L15 | Lookup training on fictional entities teaches reading over recalling at 20-60M | The only way to answer a fictional-entity question is to read the result; counterfactual tests expose memorization | Train the lookup slice; test with results that contradict common knowledge and with empty results | At least 90% of answers follow the result, and at least 90% of empty results are reported as not found |
| L16 | More skill events per conversation (3 vs 1) improves probes at equal tokens | Skill density raises the fraction of tokens that carry the skill | Two renders of equal token count with 1 vs 3 events per conversation | 3-event data at least +5 on probes with no naturalness drop in a blind read |
| L17 | A distance curriculum (short gaps first) speeds learning | Intuition only; BabyLM curricula mostly did not help, so the prior is negative | Uniform vs increasing distance schedule | At least 5 points faster to threshold; expected result is no difference, which would close the idea |

## Open questions

1. Does script-mode data, where the teacher is told every answer, teach the same thing as live-mode data where the teacher had to remember? L13 tests it; nothing published speaks to it.
2. How does a model trained on a controlled register handle real, messy user input? The final "real conversation test" will contain out-of-list words, typos and topics outside the skeleton set (L4).
3. Is ufakzeka-1's null result at 151M (multi-turn tracking unmoved by data) about its data, its Turkish setting, its 90 tokens per parameter, or a real capacity limit? A single project, but it is the only published measurement at Planck's size.
4. Real throughput and memory fit of each teacher on this exact M5 (24 GB, with another job running), and whether 26B-A4B fits under the default Metal limit at a useful batch size without raising `iogpu.wired_limit_mb`.
5. Is Gemma 4's text path fp16-safe on Turing? Only the vision tower has a reported overflow, but no Turing measurement exists.
6. Does the eval stay honest if the same teacher renders training data and eval conversations? Rendering the sealed eval with a different model family, and checking n-gram overlap, is the proposed guard; whether style overlap still inflates scores is unmeasured.
7. What is the right slot-value mix between common names, rare real names and nonce strings for transfer to real users (L5)?
8. How much general narrative text does a 10-20M chat model need for fluency, and does TinyStories itself (GPT-generated, possibly restrictive terms) or an open-teacher rebuild (LlamaTales-style) serve better?
9. Should the lookup tool format be in the 10-20M model at all, or only from 60M up?
10. Licensing of any borrowed data: TinyChat is CC-BY-NC-4.0 and GPT-4o-mini output; TinyStories and TinyDialogues are GPT outputs. Apache-2.0 teachers (Gemma 4, Ministral 3, Qwen3.5) avoid the question for newly generated data.
